# Source material — Big Five (IPIP-NEO-120)

> **Part 1** below is the verbatim item source (unchanged). **Part 2**, appended at the end, documents the v2 hierarchy, norms, and derived statistics.

**Instrument id:** `bigfive`  
**Construct:** Five Factor Model — 5 domains x 6 facets x 4 items = 120 items.

## Provenance (public domain, cross-checked)

- **Item pool:** International Personality Item Pool (IPIP) — public domain (no permission/fee). https://ipip.ori.org/newPermission.htm
- **Instrument:** IPIP-NEO-120. **Citation:** Johnson, J. A. (2014). Development of the IPIP-NEO-120. *Journal of Research in Personality, 51*, 78-89.
- **Verbatim items cross-checked across two independent public-domain repositories:**
  - Alheimsins/b5-johnson-120-ipip-neo-pi-r (data/en/questions.json) — provides text + keyed + domain + facet.
  - NeuroQuestAi/five-factor-e (data/IPIP-NEO/120/questions.json) — independent item list.
  - 119/120 items identical; **1 one-word variant** (item O6 below): Alheimsins "…no absolute right **and** wrong" vs NeuroQuest "…right **or** wrong". This file uses the canonical IPIP wording ("and"). _Confirm against primary source in content-fidelity check._
  - Sampled items also matched ipip.ori.org/30FacetNEO-PI-RItems.htm.

## Scoring

5-point: Very Inaccurate(0) … Very Accurate(4). Reverse-keyed items (keyed = minus) are flipped. Each facet = sum of its 4 items (range 0–16); each domain = sum of its 6 facets (range 0–96). **From v2.0.0 the bands are norm-referenced percentiles across a four-level hierarchy — see Part 2.**

# Part 2 — Hierarchy and norms (v2.0.0)

Version 1 reported 35 scales as three criterion-referenced bands: the raw sum was
divided by the maximum possible sum and split at thirds. That has one fatal
property in a classroom — because the average person answers well above the
midpoint of a Likert scale, almost every student lands in the middle band on
almost every scale, so the instrument reports "balanced" and teaches nothing.
Version 2 changes two things: **what is reported** (four hierarchy levels instead
of two) and **what a band means** (position among people, not position on the
answer scale).

## 2.1 The hierarchy

Personality structure is hierarchical, not flat. This instrument now reports all
four published levels from one set of 120 answers.

| Level | n | What it is | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metatrait | 2 | Stability, Plasticity | Digman (1997); DeYoung (2006) |
| Domain | 5 | The Big Five | Costa & McCrae; IPIP-NEO-120 (Johnson, 2014) |
| Aspect | 10 | Two per domain | DeYoung, Quilty & Peterson (2007) |
| Facet | 30 | Six per domain, 4 items each | IPIP-NEO-120 (Johnson, 2014) |

**Metatraits.** Stability = −Neuroticism + Agreeableness + Conscientiousness.
Plasticity = Extraversion + Openness. These are the shared-variance factors above
the Big Five; DeYoung (2006) gives the two-factor account used here.

**Aspects.** DeYoung, Quilty & Peterson (2007) factor-analysed 75 facet scales
and found exactly two factors inside each domain. They also note that where an
inventory does not include the Big Five Aspect Scales themselves, the Table 3
loadings can be used to pick NEO facets as aspect markers — which is what this
instrument does.

### The membership rule

One rule, applied uniformly, so that every inclusion and exclusion is auditable:

> A facet marks an aspect when its loading in DeYoung et al. (2007) Table 3 is
> **≥ .50** *and* **higher than its loading on the sibling aspect** of the same
> domain. An aspect score is the unit-weighted sum of its marker facets.

Four facets — **Excitement-Seeking, Liberalism, Trust, Modesty** — mark neither
aspect, because they do not discriminate between the two halves of their domain.
They are still reported as facets and still contribute to their domain; they just
do not enter an aspect score. This is a finding, not a gap.

### Known limitations of this mapping (report them to students)

- **Intellect** and **Orderliness** each rest on a **single facet** (4 items).
  DeYoung et al. found that NEO-style facets simply do not carry two clean
  markers for these aspects. Their uncertainty ranges are correspondingly wide,
  and the read-out says so.
- **Compassion** is the weakest of the ten. DeYoung et al. state plainly that no
  good Compassion markers appear in the NEO-PI-R: Altruism loads .65 on
  Compassion and .64 on Politeness, so it barely discriminates. Read it with
  caution.
- **Openness** is the loosest domain overall, consistent with Kajonius & Johnson
  (2019), who found Imagination, Emotionality and Liberalism only weakly related
  to the general Openness factor. Our back-solved average facet intercorrelation
  reproduces this independently: Openness is the lowest of the five (see §2.3).

## 2.2 Norms

Scores are placed against published age- and sex-specific norms from **Johnson's
IPIP-NEO data repository**, the same tables distributed with the IPIP-NEO scoring
programs. Four comparison groups ship with the instrument, and the student can
switch between them on the results page:

| Group id | Comparison group | Default |
|---|---|---|
| `u21-all` | People under 21, male and female norms averaged | ✔ |
| `u21-male` | Males under 21 | |
| `u21-female` | Females under 21 | |
| `adult-all` | Adults 21–40, male and female norms averaged | |

`u21-all` is the default because the students are 16–17 and because it asks
nothing personal. The switcher is deliberate teaching: a percentile is
meaningless without a stated comparison group, and watching every number move
when the group changes makes that concrete.

**Metric conversion.** The published tables are on the 1–5 response metric
(facet 4–20, domain 24–120). This app scores responses 0–4. Means are therefore
shifted down by one point per item — **facet −4, domain −24** — and standard
deviations are unchanged. Nothing else about the published values is altered.

**Percentile.** percentile = Φ((score − mean) / SD), clamped to 1–99. The
normal-distribution assumption is the standard one for these tables; it is a
simplification, which is part of why every number ships with an uncertainty
range rather than as a point.

## 2.3 Derived quantities (what is computed rather than published)

Published tables give means and SDs for the 5 domains and 30 facets only. The
aspect and metatrait norms are derived, as follows, and the derivations are
recorded here so a psychologist can check them.

**Aspect and metatrait means** are exact: the mean of a sum is the sum of the
means, with sign. No assumption involved.

**Aspect SDs.** For a unit-weighted sum, Var = Σ Var_i + 2 Σ_{i<j} r · SD_i · SD_j.
The average within-domain facet correlation *r* is **back-solved from the norm
table itself**, using the identity that the six facets of a domain sum to that
domain:

    r = (Var(domain) − Σ Var(facet_i)) / (2 · Σ_{i<j} SD_i · SD_j)

For `u21-all` this yields N .37, E .39, O .26, A .33, C .41 — all inside the
published range for NEO facet intercorrelations, and with Openness lowest, which
independently reproduces Kajonius & Johnson's (2019) structural finding. The one
assumption is that the covariance is homogeneous across facet pairs within a
domain.

**Metatrait SDs** use the same formula over domains, with an **assumed** average
domain intercorrelation of **.25** (Stability) and **.24** (Plasticity), which
are typical published magnitudes for Big Five intercorrelations (e.g. van der
Linden, te Nijenhuis & Bakker, 2010). Unlike the facet correlation, this one is
not back-solved from the table — so the two metatrait percentiles are the most
approximate numbers in the read-out.

**Reliability and the uncertainty range.** Johnson (2014) reports domain alphas
(N .90, E .89, O .81, A .86, C .90) and facet alpha *ranges* per domain but not
individual facet alphas. Each facet therefore takes the **low end** of its
domain's published range — N .69, E .69, O .63, A .71, C .67 — a deliberately
conservative choice. Composite reliabilities use the standard formula

    ρ = 1 − Σ SD_i²(1 − α_i) / SD_composite²

As a check, running that formula over the facets reproduces the published domain
alphas within about .03 (e.g. reconstructed N .89 vs published .90), which
supports the conservative facet-alpha floor. The read-out shows a percentile
interval of **one standard error of measurement** either side of the observed
score, where SEM = SD · √(1 − ρ).

## 2.4 Bands

Seven bands, cut on percentile:

| Band | Percentile | Copy pole |
|---|---|---|
| `very-low` | < 10 | low |
| `low` | 10 – 25 | low |
| `slightly-low` | 25 – 40 | low |
| `mid` | 40 – 60 | mid |
| `slightly-high` | 60 – 75 | high |
| `high` | 75 – 90 | high |
| `very-high` | ≥ 90 | high |

Seven display bands share three sets of authored copy (`copy_poles` in the
definition), so the interpretive text stays reviewable at 47 scales × 3 poles
rather than 47 × 7. The middle band is now 20 percentile points wide instead of
absorbing the bulk of the distribution.
